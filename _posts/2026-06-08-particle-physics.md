Particle physics describes matter in terms of fundamental particles known as **quarks** and **leptons**. Quarks combine through the strong interaction to form composite particles called **hadrons**.

Hadrons are broadly classified into two families:

1. **Baryons** — composed of three quarks.
2. **Mesons** — composed of a quark-antiquark pair.

Understanding the quark content of these particles is a common topic in undergraduate examinations.

---

# The Six Quark Flavors

The Standard Model contains six quark flavors:

| Symbol | Name    | Electric Charge |
| ------ | ------- | --------------- |
| (u)    | Up      | $(+\frac{2}{3}e)$ |
| (d)    | Down    | $(-\frac{1}{3}e)$ |
| (s)    | Strange | $(-\frac{1}{3}e)$ |
| (c)    | Charm   | $(+\frac{2}{3}e)$ |
| (b)    | Bottom  | $(-\frac{1}{3}e)$ |
| (t)    | Top     | $(+\frac{2}{3}e)$ |

Each quark has a corresponding antiquark:

$$
\bar{u},\ \bar{d},\ \bar{s},\ \bar{c},\ \bar{b},\ \bar{t}
$$

with opposite electric charge and quantum numbers.

---

# Nucleons

Nucleons are the particles that make up atomic nuclei.

There are two nucleons:

| Particle      | Quark Content | Charge |
| ------------- | ------------- | ------ |
| Proton ((p))  | (uud)         | (+1e)  |
| Neutron ((n)) | (udd)         | (0)    |

### Proton

The proton consists of

$$
p = uud
$$

The total charge is

$$
+\frac{2}{3}
+\frac{2}{3}
-\frac{1}{3}
= +1.
$$

### Neutron

The neutron consists of

$$
n = udd
$$

The total charge is

$$
+\frac{2}{3}
-\frac{1}{3}
-\frac{1}{3}
=0.
$$

These two particles form the building blocks of ordinary matter.

---

# Baryon Family

A **baryon** is any hadron composed of three quarks:

$$
qqq
$$

All baryons have baryon number

$$
B=+1.
$$

The nucleons are the lightest baryons, but many heavier baryons also exist.

## Common Baryons

| Particle    | Symbol      | Quark Content | Charge |
| ----------- | ----------- | ------------- | ------ |
| Proton      | $(p)$         | (uud)         | +1     |
| Neutron     | $(n)$         | (udd)         | 0      |
| Lambda      | $(\Lambda^0)$ | (uds)         | 0      |
| Sigma Plus  | $(\Sigma^+)$  | (uus)         | +1     |
| Sigma Zero  | $(\Sigma^0)$  | (uds)         | 0      |
| Sigma Minus | $(\Sigma^-)$  | (dds)         | -1     |
| Xi Zero     | $(\Xi^0)$     | (uss)         | 0      |
| Xi Minus    | $(\Xi^-)$     | (dss)         | -1     |
| Omega Minus | $(\Omega^-)$  | (sss)         | -1     |

These particles are often referred to as **hyperons** when they contain one or more strange quarks.

---

# Meson Family

Mesons consist of a quark and an antiquark:

$$
q\bar q
$$

Mesons have baryon number

$$
B=0.
$$

They are generally unstable and play an important role in mediating strong interactions inside nuclei.

---

## Pions ((\pi)-Mesons)

The pion family is the lightest meson family.

| Particle | Quark Content                   | Charge |
| -------- | ------------------------------- | ------ |
| $(\pi^+)$  | $(u\bar d)$                       | +1     |
| $(\pi^-)$  | $(d\bar u)$                       | -1     |
| $(\pi^0)$  | $(u\bar u)$ and $(d\bar d)$ mixture | 0      |

Pions are commonly produced in nuclear and cosmic-ray interactions.

---

## Kaons

Kaons contain a strange quark or antiquark.

| Particle   | Quark Content | Charge |
| ---------- | ------------- | ------ |
| $(K^+)$     | $(u\bar s)$     | +1     |
| $(K^-)$      | $(s\bar u)$     | -1     |
| $(K^0)$      | $(d\bar s)$     | 0      |
| $(\bar K^0)$ | $(s\bar d)$     | 0      |

Kaons were historically important in the discovery of strange particles and CP violation.

---

# Antibaryon Family

For every baryon there exists an antibaryon composed entirely of antiquarks:

$$
\bar q \bar q \bar q
$$

Antibaryons carry baryon number

$$
B=-1.
$$

---

## Common Antibaryons

| Particle               | Quark Content        |
| ---------------------- | -------------------- |
| Antiproton ($(\bar p)$)  | $(\bar u\bar u\bar d)$ |
| Antineutron ($(\bar n)$) | $(\bar u\bar d\bar d)$ |
| Anti-Lambda            | $(\bar u\bar d\bar s)$ |
| Anti-Sigma Plus        | $(\bar u\bar u\bar s)$ |
| Anti-Xi Minus          | $(\bar d\bar s\bar s)$ |

When a baryon encounters its corresponding antibaryon, they can annihilate:

$$
p+\bar p \rightarrow \gamma+\gamma+\cdots
$$

converting their mass into energy according to

$$
E=mc^2.
$$

---

# Quick Comparison

| Family      | Composition          | Baryon Number |
| ----------- | -------------------- | ------------- |
| Nucleons    | $(uud,\ udd)$          | +1            |
| Baryons     | $(qqq)$                | +1            |
| Mesons      | $(q\bar q)$            | 0             |
| Antibaryons | $(\bar q\bar q\bar q)$ | -1            |

---

# Important Exam Facts

### Remember:

* Proton = (uud)
* Neutron = (udd)
* Antiproton = (\bar u\bar u\bar d)
* Meson = quark + antiquark
* Baryon = three quarks
* Antibaryon = three antiquarks
* Pion is the lightest meson.
* Proton is the only stable baryon in free space.
* Strange baryons are called **hyperons**.
* Mesons have baryon number zero.

---

# One-Line Memory Trick

> **Baryons = 3 quarks, Mesons = 2 quarks (quark + antiquark), Antibaryons = 3 antiquarks.**

If you can remember the proton ((uud)) and neutron ((udd)), most undergraduate particle-composition questions become straightforward.
